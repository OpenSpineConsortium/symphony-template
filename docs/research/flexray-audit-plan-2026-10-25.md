## 1. FleXray, from its public code and cards

Read in the fork and on the model and dataset cards (huggingface.co/VictorButoi/flexray and VictorButoi/flexray-data) and the project page (flexray.csail.mit.edu). Each fact names where it was read so the audit threads can start from it rather than read the fork again.

- One network, one head. A 2D residual UNet (src/fxr/models/unet.py), filters 64 to 1024 over seven levels, one input channel and 61 output channels under a sigmoid: 60 structures and background, the packaged model label space of the protocol all_structures_flexray_v4 (src/fxr/configs/protocols/all_structures_flexray_v4.yml, docs/protocols.md). The protocol has 63 labels; the two aggregates lumbar_spine and thoracolumbar_spine are left out of the head and declared in the eval contract as unions of vertebra channels, though the fork's own evaluator never computes the union and its training remap sends DeepFluoro's stored lumbar_spine label to background, so the paper's DeepFluoro number is not reproduced by the fork's evaluation as shipped; the project's benchmark scorer makes the union itself.
- One channel per vertebra. vertebra_c1 to c7, t1 to t12, l1 to l5 and sacrum are each a channel. There is no channel for a sixth lumbar or a thirteenth thoracic body: the MOOSE, HANSeg and ShoulderCT dataset specs list vertebra_l6, rib_left_13 and rib_right_13 under protocol_drop_labels, which compiles them to background (docs/protocols.md, "Dataset Specs"); the last two are ribs, and no spec stores a thirteenth thoracic vertebra, so how a T13 body in a training CT was labelled is not found in the fork. In the standard loss route the L6 pixels are then a hard negative for every vertebra channel in the pixel cross-entropy, and in the Dice term for the vertebra channels present in that image, which ignores empty labels, so the released weights were trained to call a sixth lumbar body background.
- Inference at 256 pixels. Every image is padded to a square and resized to the bundle's 256 (src/fxr/inference/preprocessing.py), with no tiling and no mapping of the output back to the input grid beyond the content box; the network is fully convolutional and its public classes take another size, so a windowed helper is small (item 12). Test-time augmentation (a flip and intensity changes only) and the five-member ensemble (flux000, flux025, flux0375 the flagship, flux050, flux075; about 1.9 GiB in all) are optional, at five forward passes per augmented pass (MODEL_ZOO.md, docs/inference.md, src/fxr/inference/tta.py). A multi-frame DICOM is refused, so video is scored frame by frame. Inference scales a PNG or TIFF integer file by its dtype's range and a DICOM by its own range, then applies whatever per-sample normalisation the bundle declares; training normalised each image by its own 0.5 to 99.5 percentiles, which the docs call equivalent on an unpadded image, so the stress test reads the released preprocessing.json, which the fork does not carry, and measures both scalings on 16-bit fluoroscopy frames, padded and not.
- The training mix (src/fxr/configs/training/base.yml, dataloader.proportions). Ninety-six percent of every epoch is synthetic: CT volumes rendered to DRRs on the fly at 0.585 (MOOSE 3.0 at 0.375, ShoulderCT and HANSeg at 0.05 each, PedsCT and RSNAFrac at 0.04, ElbowCT at 0.03) and FluXray, the edited DRR set, at 0.375; the four real radiograph sets, hands, feet, forearms and humeri, are one percent each. Losses are routed per dataset: a SoftDice over the channels present in the image plus a binary pixel cross-entropy over every channel for fully labelled sets, and a partial-label route that ignores the channels an image does not annotate (DatasetRoutedLoss, docs/losses.md). Two thousand epochs of 500 iterations, AdamW at 3e-4 with cosine decay, an EMA of the weights, exactly one GPU and one process (no distributed training), Weights and Biases required as a package but honoured in offline or disabled mode (docs/training.md, docs/experiment.md, the launch and experiment code). No file states the released run's hardware or duration.
- Where the per-vertebra supervision comes from. Every per-vertebra label the model learns is a CT label projected into a DRR (MOOSE from C1 to L5 and the sacrum; HANSeg and ShoulderCT carry MOOSE's label table, so their specs list the same levels whether or not a head-and-neck or shoulder scan reaches them; RSNAFrac from C1 to T7; PedsCT and ElbowCT add none) or carried by FluXray, which the dataset card says was made from MOOSE CTs; no real X-ray training set in the recipe carries a per-vertebra label. DeepFluoro and ThoracoaMonch appear only among the evaluation sets, and their specs store the lumbar and the thoracolumbar spine as one label each (src/fxr/configs/datasets/DeepFluoro.yml and ThoracoaMonch.yml, the eval contract), each scored by a union the fork's evaluator does not compute. No spine fluoroscopy set, and no set named C-ArmLSpine, appears in the fork's configs; the configs do not list MOOSE 3.0's sites, so whether any C-ArmLSpine volume reached training inside MOOSE or FluXray, the public benchmark's open question, is settled only by item 6's census by file.
- The DRR path (docs/drr.md, docs/camera.md, src/fxr/drr/poses.py). The renderer is nanodrr (pinned at or above 0.1.5 in the train extra). MOOSE's profile, 37.5 percent of batches, samples alpha in [-180, 180] degrees and beta in [-30, 30] with gamma 0, the isocenter on a random labelled structure offset by up to 100 mm across the body, a source-to-detector distance of 1150 to 1250 mm, a detector pixel of 1.25 to 2.25 mm (320 to 576 mm at the detector) and a source 500 to 900 mm from the isocenter: its cone-beam nine tenths frame 128 to 451 mm at the isocenter (0.5 to 1.76 mm a pixel, magnification 1.28 to 2.5) and its orthographic tenth 320 to 576 mm. The five other CT profiles centre the volume, are cone only but for PedsCT, roll up to plus or minus 45 degrees in four of them, and frame their own ranges, HANSeg down to 0.40 mm a pixel (102 mm). A quarter of renders are then rescaled 0.8 to 1.2 in plane, so the training stream spans about 0.33 to 2.8 mm a pixel, 85 to 720 mm. Half the time a truncated lognormal factor, above 1 on about three draws in four, multiplies the attenuation of every structure the model supervises, organs as well as bone (src/fxr/configs/training/base.yml, the drr_model profiles; src/fxr/configs/augmentations/CT_base.yml). The image is the line integral of attenuation, with no physical noise, scatter, intensifier distortion, collimation circle or grid; the augmentation then inverts it half the time, adds Gaussian noise of 0.01 on a quarter of images, changes contrast, gamma and brightness, warps it elastically, crops one axis and zero-pads it back, flips it and turns it in plane by up to 90 degrees, so the model is blind to laterality. A training render is made from one axial crop of 256 slices of a CT (the packing's crop of 512 by 512 by 256), so how much of a column one render shows depends on the slice spacing. The fixed presets are frontal, lateral, lateral_left, offangle (45 degrees) and above (45 degrees of tilt). The pose geometry was read in nanodrr 0.1.5's and roma 1.5.6's sources, the versions the fork's lock file pins.
- What the fork renders and what it does not. fxr-render writes DRRs, each min-max scaled to [0, 1] and, half the time unless drr_model's attenuation_cfg.prob is set to 0, with the training's per-label attenuation multipliers applied, with their masks and camera metadata, from a packed CT set (a ct-seg ThunderDB), with no weights and under the code's MIT licence (src/fxr/launch/render_cli.py, src/fxr/launch/render.py, docs/camera.md). It reads the pack's stored_labels, which a dense CT pack always records, and falls back to the spec of --dataset-name only when they are missing, so a stored name outside the protocol, vertebra_l6 among them, is refused until the protocol names it, and one outside the model label list renders as background without warning; its manifest records each view's pose relative to the isocenter but not the isocenter, so under MOOSE's random-label isocenter a render cannot be placed back in the CT's frame from the manifest alone. The packer does not resample, and a crop's in-plane size must equal the volume's own, so CTs packed together are brought to one in-plane size first, by padding or cropping, which keeps the voxel spacing (512 by 512 matches the released packs). The generative edit that turns a raw DRR into a FluXray image, a FLUX.2 editing step by the paper, is not in the repository: docs/camera.md, docs/drr.md and the docstring of src/fxr/launch/render.py say there is no Flux rendering, raw DRRs only; CHANGELOG.md 1.0.2 says the ONNX and bundle exporters are maintained in OmniAX, and docs/inference.md that there is no exporter from a training checkpoint to an inference bundle. FluXray itself is in the dataset: 138,063 edited DRRs from 1,597 MOOSE CTs at 90 poses each, by the dataset card. The paper gives the 90 poses: five orbit angles (0, plus or minus 45 and plus or minus 90 degrees) at three source distances and six positions along the body, with no tilt, rendered at 1024 pixels and resized to 512; about 4 percent of the edited images were filtered out for drifting from their raw render (arXiv 2609.26756, appendix A).
- Fine-tuning (docs/training.md, src/fxr/experiment/initialization.py). fxr-train --init-from starts from the released weights, from the Hugging Face id or from a local bundle directory with no network; without --replace-head the label list must match the 61 channels exactly and every tensor is copied; with it the head, the final 1x1 convolution alone, is discarded whole and freshly initialised at the new size, and --freeze-backbone trains that head alone. There is no row-wise copy that keeps 61 trained channels while adding two. A model label must belong to the protocol, so adding vertebra_l6 and vertebra_t13 needs a protocol and a model label list naming both; these can sit outside the fork under protocol.config_root, passed with --set, while the row-wise copy is code and the fork's work (item 12). A label left out of the model list renders and trains as background without warning. The training path does take real X-ray frames with channel masks for only some structures, through the partial-label route, so real frames with a few level masks can supervise it.
- Licences. The code is MIT (LICENSE, pyproject.toml). The weights are CC BY-NC 4.0, research use only, not a medical device (the model card). The dataset card gives FluXray as CC BY-NC 4.0, each image inheriting its MOOSE site licence, and the redistributed real folders as CC BY 4.0, HaN-Seg as CC BY-NC-ND. The renderer's own dependencies are permissive: nanodrr is MIT by its repository, though its 0.1.5 release ships no licence file and its PyPI pages carry no licence field; roma is BSD 3-Clause and thunderpack MIT; nanodrr's own dependencies (torchio, jaxtyping, platformdirs) were not read. The generator is not. The paper names FLUX.2 [klein] 9B as the editing model, with four denoising steps and a prompt that asks for a real X-ray's look with the anatomy and its registration unchanged (appendix A); those weights are under the FLUX Non-Commercial License v2.1, while FLUX.2 [klein] 4B and the FLUX.2 VAE are Apache 2.0 (Black Forest Labs' model cards). That licence lets output be used for any purpose in one clause (2(d)) and forbids commercial use of any data the model produces in another (4(a)), so FluXray's images carry a second non-commercial term beside their CC BY-NC 4.0 (item 15).
- The project page's claims, to be reproduced or set beside the record: mean Dice 0.875 over eight held-out sets; DeepFluoro Dice 0.916 against 0.866 for nnU-Net; a Cobb angle error of 5.13 degrees on AASCE with no failures; 2D-3D registration on 213 DeepFluoro frames at a median of 1.2 mm; "standard and non-standard acquisition angles". The fork carries no Cobb-angle, 2D-3D registration or AASCE code or spec, so those two claims can only be set beside the record, or reproduced with code from outside the fork. The paper's own angle result qualifies the last: on DeepFluoro the lumbar spine's Dice falls from 0.840 within 15 degrees of frontal (136 frames) to 0.751 beyond 25 degrees (27 frames), the one structure that declines by more than 0.05.

## 2. The owner's two questions, answered from the code

### 2.1 Does FleXray classify individual vertebral instances?

It names levels, and it names them by class, not by instance. Each vertebra is a channel of its own, so a lateral or frontal frame comes back with a mask per level, and a body is whichever level's mask covers it. That is level naming from one frame, which is the task. What it is not is instance segmentation: nothing separates two regions that light the same channel, nothing orders the channels (an L3 above an L4 is not enforced), and there is no channel at all for a sixth lumbar or a thirteenth thoracic body. Worse than absent, a sixth lumbar body was trained as background, because the CT sets that label it drop the label before training; no spec stores a thirteenth thoracic vertebra, so how one was labelled in training is not found in the fork. So on a normal spine FleXray answers the level question; on a transitional one it has no right answer for the extra body, which training pushed to background, so the expected error is a missed body, and whether the levels beside it are named wrong too is item 9's to measure. The public benchmark scored it zero-shot on a held fluoroscopy set (SchwingNet's record, confidential); the companion note sets that result beside the record and says what it does and does not show.

### 2.2 The idea: FleXray's generation for anomalous spines, and the paired corpus against its weakness in angle

Three corrections to the premise, each from the fork, and then where the idea holds.

- The renderer is not short of angles; the realistic images are. The released profiles draw the orbit angle from the full circle and the tilt from plus or minus 30 degrees, and four of the six CT profiles roll the image too, so a frontal, a lateral and every oblique in between are in the raw DRR training stream. FluXray, the edited, realistic set, holds five orbit angles and no tilt, so a C-arm tilted toward the head or the feet is seen only in raw renders. The weakness the owner names is in the paper's own numbers: on DeepFluoro the lumbar spine falls from 0.840 Dice near frontal to 0.751 beyond 25 degrees, on 27 frames. The likelier cause is realism and context at those views rather than geometry, and the audit measures it by angle on held frames (item 9) rather than assuming either.
- Nothing real supervises a level. Every per-vertebra label comes from CT. Real frames are four percent of training and carry no level (other anatomy), and the two real spine sets, DeepFluoro (lumbar_spine) and ThoracoaMonch (thoracolumbar_spine), are evaluation only, each with one label for its spine. The code can take real frames with masks for a few levels through its partial-label route; the recipe has none to give it. The paired corpus this project holds, a CT and real frames at known pose, is the one kind of data that can put a real frame and a per-vertebra label together, by projecting the CT's labels through the recorded pose, which is how FleXray's own DeepFluoro evaluation labels were made.
- Fluoroscopy's physics is absent, though its field is not. The renderer has no intensifier distortion, collimation circle, physical noise or scatter; the augmentation adds image-space noise, contrast and a one-axis crop, not a device. The fields it frames run from about 85 to 720 mm across its CT profiles and the in-plane rescale, so a nine-inch intensifier's field of about 14 cm is well inside them; a whole spine's 700 mm or more is at their edge, but no training render holds a whole column, since each is made from one 256-slice crop.

Where the idea holds. Rendering from CTs with known, labelled anomalies is the clean route to rare-class X-rays: fxr-render takes a packed CT set once the protocol names the extra labels, needs no weights and is MIT, so its output is free of the weights' licence and takes the class of the CTs it is made from; the training sampler can weight a crop-backed CT set's crops by the inverse frequency of their model classes, within that set, where its sample_weighting says so; a rare-label CT set added this way needs that setting, a share in dataloader.proportions and the rare label among the model labels, while rendered X-rays from fxr-render enter as an X-ray source with no such weighting. For the paired sets the renders must be made at each real frame's recorded pose and placed in the CT's frame, which needs two small changes to fxr-render (item 12). The generative edit that gives FluXray its realism is not in the code; the paper gives its model, its prompt and its filters, so the project's realism step is its own, or a rebuild on the Apache 2.0 klein 4B, or FluXray's images as they are, under their two non-commercial terms. The paired sets serve the angle question twice: as a measurement of FleXray per angle on real frames, and as supervision, a real frame beside a render of the same CT at the same pose. They differ more than the premise allows. C-ArmLSpine pairs each case's CBCT with its spot frames, and two of its cases with whole CBCT sweeps at known gantry angles. DeepFluoro is six cadavers with released poses and one label for the whole lumbar spine. COBRA is not a spine set: it is pelvic radiotherapy CBCT with a planning CT per case, its arcs labelled full or half rather than a uniform 360 degrees, so its field holds the sacrum and at most the lowest lumbar bodies, where a lumbosacral transition sits, but not a whole column. LUMOS pairs CTs with diagnostic radiographs at no recorded pose, so it needs registration first, and Ljubljana is one cadaver. The table of every held pair is docs/research/paired-ct-xray-annotation-plan.md. COBRA, DeepFluoro and LUMOS are non-commercial, which touches item 15 only once a SchwingNet network trains on them; measuring with them is free of it.

## 4. Item 15: the licence and rights, laid out for the owner

Facts first. FleXray's code is MIT, and its renderer's dependencies are permissive (nanodrr MIT by its repository, though its 0.1.5 release ships no licence file; roma BSD 3-Clause; thunderpack MIT), so the fork and fxr-render are the project's to use and to license, and every render made with them is free of the weights' licence and takes the class of the CTs it is made from. The held CTs that label the rare bodies are non-commercial or share-alike (CTSpinoPelvic1K, CTSpine1K, VerSe), so a rare-class set for anything the filing touches needs anomaly CTs under a permissive class. FleXray's weights are CC BY-NC 4.0: a network fine-tuned from them is an adaptation of the weights under that licence; whether a network trained on masks they produced is one too is a question for counsel, and a network trained on FluXray's edited images is bound by FluXray's own terms. FluXray carries two non-commercial terms: CC BY-NC 4.0 as a collection, and the FLUX Non-Commercial License v2.1 of the klein 9B model that edited it, whose section 4(a) forbids commercial use of data the model produces though its section 2(d) lets output be used for any purpose. A public discussion on the model's Hugging Face page raises that conflict and Black Forest Labs has not answered it there, so it is a question for counsel. FLUX.2 [klein] 4B is Apache 2.0, the one variant whose output the project could own outright. SchwingNet's fetch list keeps FleXray's weights a benchmark row only, never in a SchwingNet network; its licence file has no entry yet for the weights or for FluXray, and only the owner loosens a class (tools/licences.py). The provisional in preparation is the other side: a network the filing claims should not rest on weights or images the project may not license.

Three options, as #391 states them, none of which blocks the audit:

1. Train under the non-commercial class. Fine-tune from FleXray's weights and use FluXray as it is; the resulting network is research-only and stays out of the filing's claims. Fastest; it answers the scientific question and nothing more.
2. Rebuild from the published method on permissive data. Render with fxr-render (MIT) from the project's own and permissively licensed CTs, train FleXray's architecture from scratch on the project's recipe, add the rare-class channels from the start, with a realism step of the project's own or on klein 4B. Slowest; the result is the project's own and can be claimed and licensed.
3. Loosen the class in the owner's own words. Record the decision in SchwingNet's licence file and proceed as in option 1 for the project's internal use, accepting that the network is not licensable.

The thread recommends 2 for anything the filing or a licence will touch and 1 for the audit's own experiments, kept apart by the licence classes the workflow already enforces. The choice also decides whether the paired sets with non-commercial terms (DeepFluoro, COBRA) may supervise a SchwingNet network or only measure it. The owner may want the independent patent attorney's reading before 2 November; the facts above are what to give them.
